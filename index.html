One tile, turned

In 1704 the Dominican priest Sébastien Truchet, who designed canals and fountains for Louis XIV, published a study of a single square tile, split along its diagonal into a dark and a light half. Laid in its four orientations, it makes an endless variety of patterns, and Truchet counted and catalogued them, as a mathematician would.

In 1987 the metallurgist Cyril Stanley Smith gave the idea a twist: two quarter circles joining the middles of the sides. Every arc meets the arc of the next tile, so the lines never end: laid at random, they wander into meanders, and close into loops of every size. The regions between them can always be colored with only two colors, like a checkerboard of curves.

The same game with a diagonal line gives the maze of 10 PRINT CHR$(205.5+RND(1)); : GOTO 10, one line of Commodore 64 BASIC that a book was written about in 2012. Push the Balance away from the middle and order creeps back in: long diagonals, rows of waves.
